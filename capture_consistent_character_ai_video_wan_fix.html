The WAN Stability Protocol: Fixing Temporal Identity Drift in AI Video

The Mechanics of Temporal Identity Collapse

One of the most significant barriers in professional AI filmmaking is the loss of likeness during animation. You might have a perfect character sheet, but the moment you use the WAN 2.2 Image-to-Video (I2V) pipeline, the character's face "morphs" or rebuilds into a generic version. This technical phenomenon is known as Temporal Identity Drift.

At its core, this is caused by Temporal Denoising Entropy. In the I2V process, the AI must reconcile two conflicting datasets: the static identity of your reference photo and the dynamic movement of your source video. In standard workflows, the model prioritizes the "flow" of movement. To ensure the animation looks fluid, it re-samples the facial features in every frame, effectively "forgetting" the unique anatomical markers of your character's jawline and eyes as the scene progresses.

This results in a character that looks like your reference in Frame 1, but by Frame 24, they have evolved into an entirely different person. This lack of stability is what separates amateur "clips" from cinematic assets that can be used in feature-length projects.

The I2V Bottleneck: Motion vs. Likeness

The transition from 2D image generation to 4D video synthesis introduces a high level of Latent Space Volatility. When you apply motion to a reference, the model’s internal "attention" is stretched across the temporal dimension. If your Denoising Strength is set too high, the AI treats the reference photo merely as a "suggestion" rather than a rigid template. It rebuilds the face to fit the new pose or expression, often choosing the path of least resistance—which usually results in a generic, "plastic" AI look.

To overcome this, you must implement a Consistent Latent Video Anchor. This is not a single setting but a comprehensive architecture that "clamps" the identity tokens throughout the entire animation sequence. By forcing the model to refer back to a custom-trained identity model (LoRA) at specific intervals in the denoising process, you can prevent the identity from bleeding away into motion-induced noise.

The Solution: Deterministic Identity Transfer

Achieving studio-grade stability in WAN requires a shift from simple animation to Structural Identity Injection. This involves a custom I2V workflow where the reference photo acts as a permanent anchor. Instead of letting the AI rebuild the face, we use a multi-pass approach: the first pass generates the motion, and the second "identity pass" surgically restores the character's original traits frame-by-frame.

By using a dedicated WAN 2.2 I2V Pipeline, we can control exactly how much of the original likeness is preserved. This deterministic approach ensures that whether your character is talking, running, or turning their head, their unique facial geometry—the eye shape, the specific jawline curvature, and skin texture—remains 100% locked. This turns AI video from a random experiment into a reliable tool for professional content creators and AI influencers.

Stability is the only metric that matters for narrative storytelling. When your character remains identical across 10 different shots, you no longer have a "generation"; you have a "cast member."
