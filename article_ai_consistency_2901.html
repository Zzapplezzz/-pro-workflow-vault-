Character-Locked AI Cinematography: Moving Beyond the 'Slot Machine' Prompt

Most AI videos look bad not because of the technology, but because of a fundamental misunderstanding of the workflow. As Adil noted, typing a long prompt and hitting generate is essentially playing a 'slot machine'. You have zero control over the composition, and the AI is forced to invent the subject, the movement, and the environment simultaneously, leading to the dreaded 'morphing' effect.

The 80% Rule: The Power of the Start Frame

The fix begins with the Start Frame. A start frame is where your video begins, providing the AI with a visual anchor. Without it, you are asking the machine to hallucinate physics from scratch. By using a production-ready image model like Soul 2 as your starting point, you lock in 80% of your final result before any animation takes place. Remember: garbage in, garbage out.

The Five Pillars of Control

To think like a director instead of a prompt engineer, you must master the Five Pillars of a shot: Who (Subject), Where (Location), What (Action), Camera Move, and Mood. Instead of using vague adjectives like 'cinematic,' professional creators specify clear intent. For example, selecting a 'handheld' camera movement adds a natural shake that replicates real-life physics, making the scene feel grounded and authentic.

Director's Tip: If you struggle with fast motion morphing, generate your scene in slow-motion first, then speed it up in post-production. It’s a simple trick that saves hours of failed generations.

However, the greatest challenge remains Consistency. Stitching multiple clips together only works if your actor retains the same hair color, eye color, and features across every frame. To solve 'face drift,' you need a professional character-tagging system that ensures the same persona appears in every shot, even from different angles.
